# Research copy a reader can follow

Use this skill to explain a computation to a technically curious colleague. The reference is Pathway's architecture writing, not a license to speak for Pathway or repeat its promotional conclusions. Read [the research notes](references/pathway-writing.md) before applying the skill to BDH material.

## Find the explanation before polishing sentences

Identify the reader's question, the object they can follow, and what happens to that object. A key, memory matrix, graph edge or search frontier is useful; an evidence category is not an opening scene. Preserve the actual audience and assignment, including word limits and required citations.

Reconstruct the technological argument from sources and code: the existing operation, the reason to change it, the proposed update, and the consequence. Distinguish a mathematical rearrangement from a new model, a graph interpretation from its GPU implementation, and a toy from trained behavior. Do not infer an author's private thinking or assume that corporate prose has a single author or human-only provenance.

Let the explanation follow the reader's next question. A useful progression is a concrete problem, the design choice it motivates, how that choice works, an observed result, and what to test next. It is a progression of ideas, not a mandatory five-heading template.

## Write the mechanism

- Explain an operation in ordinary language before introducing its abstraction. Say what a write changes, what a read combines, or why a search stops. Keep necessary technical names once they have been introduced.
- Give every symbol a job before displaying the equation. Break a derivation at its meaningful steps; never compress a whole proof into a caption.
- Keep the subject of each sentence visible. Prefer “the new key also matches A” to “a failure boundary is established.” Use “we” for work actually performed, “you” for a reader action, and named researchers for published findings.
- Make a paragraph advance the explanation. Vary sentence length for thought, not to manufacture a voice. A short sentence should clarify or conclude, not act as a slogan.
- Use an analogy only when its parts map to the computation. Explain where the mapping ends. Concrete technical examples often work better than borrowed metaphors.
- In an experiment, say what changes, what stays fixed, what the reader should inspect, and how to interpret the result. Do not replace this with “interrogate,” “substrate,” “invariant,” or “identifiability” unless the term itself is being taught.

## Put rigor where the reader needs it

Keep citations beside substantive published statements. Explain the important assumption at the point where omitting it would mislead. Group remaining implementation details in optional methods, source records or a reproducibility document. A limitation should name a specific unsupported inference, not repeatedly announce that the project has limitations.

Do not remove caveats to make the prose sound confident. Rewrite “not a universal capacity claim” as a description of which keys and values the result applies to. Describe whether a result was calculated now or recorded earlier in the relevant methods note; do not wallpaper a page with bordered evidence badges.

Avoid mixing a teaching page with an audit report or launch announcement. Preserve machine-readable claim records and tests when they serve reproducibility, but do not make their taxonomy the reader's navigation or the narrator's voice.

## Edit for comprehension, not an AI detector

Read the text in isolation, then next to its controls or figure. Check that a reader can answer: what does this do, what should I try, and why did the output change? Every button should describe its action. A reader should not need the methods appendix to follow the main argument. When a word limit is part of the assignment, count the final text rather than estimating its length.

Watch for recurring failure patterns: abstract noun chains; repeated “X, not Y”; manufactured “One X. Two Y.” headlines; gratuitous claims of transparency; proof vocabulary before an example; definitions that introduce three more undefined terms; footnote material enlarged into a hero headline. These are diagnostic patterns, not a banned-word list. Necessary contrasts and technical terms can stay.

Draft original prose. Do not copy distinctive Pathway sentences, metaphors, slogans or institutional authority. Preserve truthful AI disclosure; clear writing does not imply unaided authorship.

For executable or printed material, check the changed copy in its actual layout, keep the canonical essay and PDF synchronized, and rerun applicable interaction/content checks. Updating copy does not authorize a production promotion or replacing a frozen submitted artifact.
